Schools are paid for things only families can finish: a day in school, an income form, a consent. Finishing them is nobody's job. Where the two were tested side by side, help beat a letter, and help was never affordable. Now it costs a few dollars.

Schools first.

A fifth absence, a missing income form, an unsigned consent: today each one gets a letter. With Mycelium each gets an offer from the school, in the family's language: a helper that finishes it with them and makes sure what it unlocks gets used. The school pays $5.60 a student a year, and $2.40 more only if its filings show it worked.

For the offer, the helper acts as the school's official under FERPA; everything after that runs on the family's own yes. The school sees attendance and what got finished, in aggregate; never a reason.

The helper is theirs.

It finishes the whole thing.

Not just the form: the card activated, the appointment booked, the services delivered. It goes where the task goes, the county, the health plan, the state.

It answers to them.

It never tells the school a family's reasons, and no institution can pay to be recommended.

They can say no.

Nothing is filed or sent without a yes on that exact action. Stop, and it stops.

It's already in their pocket.

Text or WhatsApp, whichever they already use. Nothing to download, and nothing new for your staff.

It stays with them.

If they want it to. When a fund, a clinic or a college later offers the same person a helper, they can bring theirs.

The evidence that help, not information, is what moves outcomes:

  • Information about financial aid did nothing. Eight minutes of help filling out the form raised college enrollment 8 points.Bettinger, Long, Oreopoulos & Sanbonmatsu, randomized, 26,000 families
  • A letter raised food-assistance enrollment from 6% to 11%. A letter plus help applying raised it to 18%.Finkelstein & Notowidigdo, randomized, 31,000 seniors
  • Financial-aid reminders by text and email to more than 800,000 students changed nothing.Bird, Castleman, Denning, Goodman, Lamberton & Rosinger, randomized
  • A note home about an eye exam did nothing. Glasses handed out at school raised reading and math. A quarter of the children who needed them still went without, mostly because a permission form didn't come back.Florida vision trial, randomized, 18,000 students

No one has run this trial with families of school-age children: one helper, the family's own, finishing the form instead of mailing it. Ours is built to be it: offered school by school, measured against the schools not yet offered, so by June you know.

First we read how you're funded.

Your funding formula and your records, line by line: every dollar that depends on something in a person's life. The number comes first, free.

Then it rehearses on last year.

It runs on last year's records first. You see who it would have reached and what it would have said, then decide when it's ready.

You offer it. The family says yes.

A fifth absence, a missing form, or a member turning 65 sends an offer from you in their language. Your staff see only the cases that need a person.

Schools pay per student.

$5.60 a student a year, and $2.40 more only if numbers the district certifies to the state move: its low-income count or funded attendance. A fund pays a fifth of the premiums the state now pays instead of it.

Then anywhere money depends on a person.

We call the gap stranded alignment: outcomes an institution and a person both want, that nobody could afford to pursue one person at a time. The same helper already has a second sponsor: New York union funds, whose retirees qualify for a state program that pays their Medicare premium instead of the fund. The institutions change. The person keeps the helper, if they choose to.
