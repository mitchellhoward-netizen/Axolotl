# Health costs of a disabled, non-working fund member: what moving to Medicare saves a Taft-Hartley fund

Researched 2026-10-07. All dollar figures are restated in 2026 dollars unless marked otherwise.
Labels: **verified** means I read it in the primary source. **secondary** means it came from a
summary or search excerpt, not the primary document. **inferred** means it is my arithmetic, and the
working is shown.

## Summary (plain English)

1. The fund pays as primary for a disabled member who is no longer working. On an SSDI-type member that costs roughly **$24k (low) / $33k (middle) / $41k (high) a year** (inferred, 2026 $).
2. From month 25 of SSDI entitlement, Medicare pays first. If the fund still covers the member, it then pays only the leftovers: Medicare cost sharing, drugs (if the fund keeps Rx) and services Medicare does not cover.
3. **Primary to secondary, fund keeps Rx: savings ≈ $11.5k / $23k / $35k per member-year.**
4. **Primary to secondary, member moves to Medicare Part D (the fund wraps only cost sharing): savings ≈ $19k / $29k / $39k per member-year.**
5. **The fund ends coverage entirely at Medicare entitlement: savings ≈ $24k / $33k / $41k per member-year.** This equals the whole primary cost.
6. If the fund also reimburses the Part B premium, take **$2,435 a year** off every savings figure ($202.90 × 12, 2026).
7. Most of the gap comes from price, not from use. While primary, the fund pays commercial prices (hospitals about 2 to 2.5 times Medicare, physicians about 1.4 times). As secondary payer, the fund's liability is set on Medicare's allowed amounts.
8. The averages are driven by a few members. In Medicare FFS, the costliest 5% account for 45% of spending. In the one trial of new SSDI beneficiaries, the 12% with $50k+ in claims made up 53% of the plan's cost. **Median savings per member are probably only a third to half of the mean** (inferred). A small fund with 5 to 10 such members will see big year-to-year swings.
9. Attrition is high. About 6% of new SSDI beneficiaries die during the 24-month wait, and about 5% die in their first year on the rolls. After that, assume about 3–5% a year die plus about 1% a year recover. Five years of Medicare entitlement then yield about **4.5 expected member-years of savings** per member who reaches Medicare (inferred).
10. **Biggest caveat:** many funds stop covering a non-working member before month 25 anyway, through an exhausted hour bank or the end of COBRA. For those members the real saving is $0 unless the fund would otherwise have kept covering them. Measure this per fund (see open questions).

- Quote: "Usually 20% of the cost for each Medicare-covered service or item after you've paid your deductible".
- URL: https://www.medicare.gov/basics/costs/medicare-costs (accessed 2026-10-07).

**3. 2026 Part D deductible and out-of-pocket cap.** Label: verified.
- Quotes: "The CY 2026 annual out-of-pocket (OOP) threshold of $2,100". The enrollee pays 100% "until the deductible of $615 for CY 2026 is met."
- Fact sheet dated Apr 07, 2025.
- URL: https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/final-cy-2026-part-d-redesign-program-instructions (accessed 2026-10-07).

**4. Medicare is primary once the member is on SSDI.** Label: verified (Cornell LII copy of the CFR; eCFR redirected to a block page).
- 42 CFR 411.104(a)(2)(ii) defines "current employment status" for someone who is not working but keeps employment rights. It also requires that the person "is not receiving disability benefits from an employer for more than 6 months", "is not receiving disability benefits from Social Security", and has coverage "that is not pursuant to COBRA continuation coverage".
- The same section lists hours-bank coverage as an example of retained employment rights.
- Implication (inferred): an SSDI recipient whose fund coverage rests on an hour bank no longer has "current employment status". The disability MSP rule (large GHP primary) therefore no longer applies, and Medicare is primary.
- URL: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/411.104 (accessed 2026-10-07).

### C. Spending of new SSDI beneficiaries in the waiting period (non-Medicare pricing)

**12. Accelerated Benefits Demonstration (randomized trial, new SSDI beneficiaries without insurance; MDRC for SSA, Feb 2011).** Label: verified.
- Health plan members "averaged $19,265 in paid AB health benefit claims (including zeros ...)". Coverage averaged 11.5 months during the year.
- "12.3 percent had payments of $50,000 or more, and their payments made up 53.0 percent of the total". "Only 3.5 percent ... reached the benefit limit of $100,000." The cap truncates the tail.
- Table 3.2: inpatient $6,722; outpatient $5,611; other medical $3,741; prescription drugs **$3,093**; average copays $270.
- Paid-claims bands: $0 11.3%; $1–4,999 29.1%; $5,000–9,999 15.3%. So the **median falls in the $5,000–$9,999 band** (cumulative 55.7%; inferred), against a mean of $19,265.
- MDRC's comparison: "spending per member for the AB health plan was substantially greater than for Medicare beneficiaries who were eligible as a result of disability ... MedPAC estimated that average annual spending in 2008 per disabled Medicare beneficiary was $11,018." MDRC attributes part of the gap to "the lower cost-sharing and higher provider payments of the AB health plan compared with Medicare".
- Data period: enrollment March 2008 to January 2009, then 12 months of follow-up (about 2008–2010).
- URL: https://mdrc.org/publication/accelerated-benefits-demonstration-and-evaluation-project/file-full (Volume 1, "Impacts on Health and Employment at Twelve Months"; accessed 2026-10-07).

**13. Older waiting-period cost estimates, as cited in the ABD report.** Label: secondary (cited by MDRC; I did not read the originals).
- "For the full 24-month period, Riley estimated costs of $10,055 per person in 2000 dollars". This is Riley (2004), using 1997–2000 data, so about $5,028 a year at Medicare prices.
- "Livermore, Stapleton, and Claypool estimated the average annual costs for medical and prescription drugs to be $8,366 in 2006 dollars, based on data from 1994 to 1996."

**14. Coverage gap before SSDI.** Label: secondary (search excerpt of the Commonwealth Fund / Livermore et al. 2009 report; the page returned 403).

### E. Mortality and attrition of new SSDI beneficiaries

**18. Powell, Hartig and Jacobson, Health Affairs, March 2, 2026 (SSA data 2000–2019).**
- Verified via the Penn LDI summary: 24-month mortality of SSDI beneficiaries "ranging between 5.20% and 7.30%", against "the adjusted rate for the general population hovered around 1%". By impairment: cancer 39.3%, digestive 11.3%, blood 8.4%, HIV/AIDS 8.1%.
- Secondary (abstract via search; the Health Affairs page returned 403): "In 2019, the twenty-four-month mortality rate of SSDI beneficiaries was 5.96 percent".
- URLs: https://ldi.upenn.edu/our-work/research-updates/new-study-reveals-death-rates-during-ssdis-24-month-wait-for-medicare/ and https://www.healthaffairs.org/doi/10.1377/hlthaff.2025.00592 (accessed 2026-10-07).

**19. ABD trial, first-year deaths.** Label: verified.
- "5.4 percent (55 members) died" within the year after random assignment.
- "about 30 percent of those with a primary diagnosis of neoplasm (cancer) died in the first year, accounting for more than half of all deaths". Same URL as finding 12.

**20. McCoy and Iams, Health Care Financing Review, 1994 (1980s cohort; dated).** Label: verified.
- "Fifteen percent of the disabled workers died within 18-24 months of initial eligibility; 34 percent died within 5 years". The authors report "further losses of about 5 percent a year thereafter."
- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4193440 (accessed 2026-10-07).
- Mortality has fallen a lot since then; finding 18 is the current figure.

**21. SSA actuarial rates (all durations).** Label: secondary (ssa.gov returned 403 / Access Denied to both WebFetch and curl; figures from search excerpts).
- SSA's death-rate projection is 28.6 deaths per thousand disabled-worker beneficiaries in 2022, falling to about 23.8 per thousand by 2032 under intermediate assumptions.
- The ultimate age-sex-adjusted recovery rate is 10.8 per thousand (2024 Trustees Report).
- The 2026 assumptions raise death probabilities after "higher-than-expected 2025 disabled-worker death experience".
- SSA Actuarial Study 115: "the highest probability of death occurs within the first 2 years of entitlement"; for example, 82.14 per thousand for new 50-year-old entitlements.
- URLs: https://www.ssa.gov/oact/TR/2026/2026_Long-Range_Disability_Assumptions.pdf ; https://www.ssa.gov/oact/NOTES/as115/as115_VI_E.html ; https://www.ssa.gov/oact/NOTES/pdf_studies/study130.pdf (2016–20 select death tables, unread) (accessed 2026-10-07).

## The arithmetic (all inferred)

**Inflation index.** BLS CPI-U Medical Care (FRED series CPIMEDSL, SA): Jul 2000 261.200; Jul 2006 336.900; Jul 2008 364.156; Jul 2009 376.001; Jul 2023 547.141; Jul 2024 564.593; Jul 2026 594.393.
- Source: https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/CPIMEDSL.txt (accessed 2026-10-07).
- Factors to 2026: 2024 → **1.0528**; 2023 → 1.0864; 2009 → **1.5808**; 2008 → 1.6322; 2006 → 1.7643; 2000 → 2.2756.
- This is a price index. It leaves out growth in use and intensity, so older figures inflated with it are probably understated.

### Step 1. Total spending on a disabled member, at Medicare prices (2026 $)
- A/B Medicare payments: $13,500 × 1.0528 = $14,213.
- A/B cost sharing: $2,365 × 1.0528 = $2,490.
- A/B allowed: $16,702.
- Rx (Part D gross, under 65): $10,177 × 1.0528 = $10,714.
- **Total ≈ $27,417 per year at Medicare prices.**
- Cross-checks:
  - MedPAC disabled all-Medicare figure: $23,193 × 1.0864 = $25,196. It excludes cost sharing, so it is consistent.
  - FR 2026 Part B benefits: $12,047, against FFS Part B 2024 × 1.0528 = $9,524. The FR figure includes MA and is higher; see conflicts.

### Step 2. What the fund pays as primary (P), commercial prices, before Medicare
Assumption: the fund pays 88% of allowed cost and the member pays 12%. This is my assumption, not sourced; Taft-Hartley plans tend to be rich.

| Case | Build | P (2026 $) |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Fund pays Medicare-level prices (reference-based pricing floor): $27,417 × 0.88 | **$24,127** |
| Middle | ABD measured claims, annualised: $19,265 × 12/11.5 = $20,103 × 1.5808 = $31,778. Price build (below), mid multipliers: $34,968. Average of the two | **≈ $33,400** |
| High | Price build, high multipliers | **$41,452** |

**Price build.** Commercial multipliers are applied to 2024 disabled FFS service lines. The hospital lines are inpatient $5,147 and outpatient $4,064; physician is $4,668; other lines (SNF, hospice, home health) total $1,205, with the multiplier set to 1.0.
- Low: hospital 1.41, physician 1.18 → blended 1.306.
- Mid: 1.99 / 1.43 → 1.738.
- High: 2.53 / 1.79 → 2.179.
- Then P = (A/B allowed $16,702 × blended multiplier + Rx $10,714) × 0.88:
  - mid: (29,022 + 10,714) × 0.88 = $34,968.
  - high: (36,391 + 10,714) × 0.88 = $41,452.
- The ABD figure without annualising is $19,265 × 1.5808 = $30,455. The $100k cap and the CPI-only index both pull it down.

### Step 3. What the fund still pays as secondary (S), once Medicare is primary
Components:
- **(a) Medicare A/B cost sharing:** $2,490 (2026). The low case uses 50% ($1,245), for carve-out or non-duplication COB, which pays less than full cost sharing.
- **(b) Drugs:**
  - **Scenario A, the fund keeps Rx as primary for drugs:** low $4,890 (ABD Rx $3,093 × 1.5808); high $9,428 ($10,714 × 0.88); mid $7,160 (average).
  - **Scenario B, the member enrolls in Part D and the fund wraps cost sharing:** fund pays the Part D deductible up to the out-of-pocket cap: low $615, mid $1,800, high $2,100.
- **(c) Non-covered services** (dental, vision, hearing): low $250, mid $500, high $750. These are my assumptions, unsourced; ABD dental was only $98 a year in 2008–10 dollars.

| | Low S | Mid S | High S |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scenario A (keeps Rx) | 1,245 + 4,890 + 250 = **$6,385** | 2,490 + 7,160 + 500 = **$10,150** | 2,490 + 9,428 + 750 = **$12,668** |
| Scenario B (Part D) | 1,245 + 615 + 250 = **$2,110** | 2,490 + 1,800 + 500 = **$4,790** | 2,490 + 2,100 + 750 = **$5,340** |

### Step 4. Savings per member-year (2026 $)
Low savings pairs low P with high S. Middle pairs mid with mid. High pairs high P with low S.

| Option | Low | Middle | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary → secondary, fund keeps Rx | 24,127 − 12,668 = **$11,459** | 33,400 − 10,150 = **$23,250** | 41,452 − 6,385 = **$35,067** |
| Primary → secondary, member on Part D | 24,127 − 5,340 = **$18,787** | 33,400 − 4,790 = **$28,610** | 41,452 − 2,110 = **$39,342** |
| Fund ends coverage at Medicare entitlement | **$24,127** | **$33,400** | **$41,452** |
| Adjustment if fund reimburses Part B premium | −$2,435 | −$2,435 | −$2,435 |

As a share of primary cost, the secondary fund keeps about 69–90% of what it would otherwise spend (middle about 70% with Rx kept, about 86% with Part D).

### Step 5. Mean versus median (sensitivity)
- **ABD:** the mean is $19,265, but the median falls in the $5k–$10k band. So median ≈ 0.26–0.52 × mean; take about 0.4 as central (inferred).
- **CMS disabled FFS:** at most about 4% of enrollees (the $50k+ band) account for about 39% of payments.
- **Implication (inferred):** the *median* disabled member's saving is roughly **$8k–$14k** a year (0.4 × the middle figures), while the mean is $23k–$33k.
- For a fund with N such members, the expected saving is N × mean. The realised saving is lumpy: one cancer or transplant case can be $100k+ (MedPAC top-decile mean is $110,264).
- Price savings bigger than average multiples are concentrated in the high-cost hospital cases, where commercial and Medicare prices differ most.

### Step 6. Attrition (expected member-years of savings)
- Before Medicare starts, about 6% of SSDI beneficiaries die in the 24-month wait (finding 18). For these members savings never start, but the fund also stops paying.
- After Medicare starts, assume deaths of 3–5% a year and recoveries of about 1% a year. The all-duration death rate is about 2.9% (28.6 per 1,000, finding 21); recently awarded members run higher (finding 21: first 2 years highest; finding 20: about 5% a year in an older cohort). That gives total attrition of about 4–6% a year.
- Expected member-years over the first 5 years of Medicare = Σ_{t=0..4} (1−a)^t:
  - a = 4%: 1 + .96 + .9216 + .8847 + .8493 = **4.62**.
  - a = 6%: 1 + .94 + .8836 + .8306 + .7807 = **4.43**.
- **Example (inferred):** a member reaching Medicare at 58 with the fund keeping Rx, middle case: $23,250 × about 4.5 ≈ **$105k of expected savings over 5 years**. Low case about $52k; high case about $158k. These are before discounting and before medical trend, which roughly offset.
- Age 65 ends the comparison: the member then becomes an aged Medicare beneficiary either way.

## Open questions and conflicts

1. **Is the fund still covering the member at month 25?**
   - Many Taft-Hartley members lose eligibility when the hour bank runs out. They then elect COBRA, which has an 18-to-29-month extension for SSA-determined disability.
   - Under 42 CFR 411.104, COBRA coverage is not "current employment status", so Medicare is primary over COBRA. COBRA can also end when Medicare entitlement begins after election.
   - If the fund would have dropped the member anyway, the true saving is $0. The saving is real only where fund rules (disability extension, self-pay, retiree plan) keep the member covered.
   - **This needs fund-level data. It could cut the realised saving more than any price assumption.**
2. **Timing of SSDI awards:** the five-month SSDI waiting period, retroactive awards, and appeals lasting 1–2+ years. Medicare entitlement can be retroactive, so recovery of claims the fund already paid (provider rebilling to Medicare) is a possible extra saving I did not size.
3. **Medicare spending conflicts:**
   - CMS FFS disabled A+B is $13,500 (2024), or $14,213 in 2026 $.
   - The FR 2026 Part B figure alone is $12,047. It includes MA (61% of disabled are in managed care, MedPAC Chart 3-2) and administrative assumptions, against FFS Part B 2024 of $9,046.
   - MedPAC MCBS gives $23,193 (2023) for all Medicare including Part D. Its $1.2T total exceeds Trustees outlays because MCBS measures something different.
   - I used CMS FFS (claims-based) as the base. If the FR basis is right, the cost-sharing wrap is similar but total Medicare-priced spending would be about $2.5k higher.
4. **Rx is the largest swing in Scenario A.**
   - CMS under-65 Part D gross ($10.7k) is pre-rebate and LIS-heavy. ABD Rx ($4.9k in 2026 $) is dated and CPI-inflated, so specialty-drug growth is missed.
   - Whether the fund keeps Rx primary, uses an EGWP, or pushes members to Part D changes the saving by about $5k a year. Under-65 Medicare beneficiaries can use the Retiree Drug Subsidy or EGWP routes; I did not verify their eligibility rules here.
5. **COB method:** standard COB versus carve-out or non-duplication. A carve-out plan may pay almost nothing on top of Medicare, which pushes savings toward the high end. I modelled this only as 50% versus 100% of cost sharing.
6. **Plan cost share (88%) and non-covered services ($250–$750)** are my assumptions, not sourced. Replace them with the fund's own plan design and claims.
7. **Commercial price multiples vary a lot by market:** hospitals 141%–259% (KFF), 253% (RAND, secondary). New York (the site's market) is likely at or above the middle. A fund on reference-based pricing gets far less saving from the price gap (the "Low" P).
8. **Waiting-period spending versus spending on Medicare:**
   - New SSDI beneficiaries cost more than long-tenure disabled Medicare beneficiaries (end of life, cancer); see finding 13 and Riley (2004) as cited by MDRC.
   - Using all-duration CMS Medicare data for the secondary-phase population is reasonable. Using it to infer *primary* cost in the waiting period probably understates P, so the middle case leans on ABD.
