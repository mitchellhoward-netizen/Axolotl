# Unfinished family paperwork drains California schools

California funds its schools, staffs them and holds them legally accountable through steps that only a family can finish: getting a child to school each day, returning an income form or being matched through CalFresh or Medi-Cal by October 31, signing a special-education assessment plan, giving one-time Medicaid billing consent, filing a FAFSA or CADAA by March 2, renewing Medi-Cal, returning a glasses permission slip. Those steps fail often, in measurable ways, and most often in the poorest and most immigrant-heavy schools. Attendance is the best-measured gap. **19.4% of California students (1.14 million) were chronically absent in 2024-25**, and each absent day costs a district about $60–$90 in state aid. Other gaps are partly measured: homeless students were undercounted by at least 37% in 2017-18, about 25% of seniors neither filed for aid nor opted out in the first year of the mandate, California students left about $550M in Pell Grants unclaimed, and more than 330,000 children lost Medi-Cal, mostly over paperwork. Some gaps have never been published at all, including the statewide number of low-income students who are eligible but not counted. In the trials that tested doing the task with the person against telling them about it, doing the task won. In SNAP, enrollment went from 6% with no contact, to 11% with a letter, to 18% with help applying. For the FAFSA, information alone did nothing and help filing added 16 to 27 points. One-way texts to 800,000 students did nothing. **No randomized trial of assisted form completion for K-12 families exists.** That is the thesis's main gap in evidence, and it is also the pilot Mycelium should sell. The business is not any one form; Medicaid consent alone is worth a few million dollars a year statewide. The business is the whole edge, where one helper, acting on one family's yes, finishes several funded processes at once. A bottom-up estimate puts the revenue a 10,000-student high-need district could recover at roughly $0.2–0.55M in the first year, and up to about $1.2M a year once the three-year count pool fills. That is before counting the family's own benefits.

## California pays schools for steps only families can finish

The thesis rests on how California pays districts and how its laws assign duties. The Local Control Funding Formula (LCFF) pays mostly on average daily attendance (ADA). It adds a 20% supplemental grant and a 65% concentration grant for "unduplicated" students: low-income, English learner, foster or homeless ([CDE 2024-25 rates](https://www.cde.ca.gov/FG/aa/pa/pa2425rates.asp)). The concentration grant applies only to the share of unduplicated students above 55% of enrollment. Both money streams depend on families. A day is funded only if the child is in the seat. A low-income student is counted only if the family returns a meal application or alternative income form by October 31, or if the family is enrolled in CalFresh, CalWORKs or Medi-Cal (with income at or below 185% of the poverty line) and the state's monthly CALPADS match succeeds ([CDE Alternative Income Forms](https://www.cde.ca.gov/FG/AA/PA/altincomeforms.asp); [CDE SNP-02-2025](https://www.cde.ca.gov/LS/nu/sn/mbsnp022025.asp)). Federal money works the same way. School Medi-Cal billing requires a parent's consent "even if the student is not currently enrolled in Medi-Cal" ([LEA BOP Collaborative](https://www.tpaa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/LEA-Medi-Cal-Billing-Option-Collaborative.pdf)), and the new CYBHI mental-health fee schedule cannot bill a claim until the school has the student's insurance details, which LA County's education office calls "new for schools" and dependent on "parent or caregiver cooperation" ([CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/health/mental-health/2025/05/youth-mental-health-insurance-billing/)).

The legal clocks are built the same way. California's 60-day special-education assessment clock starts only when the parent returns signed consent ([CDE SPP/APR overview](https://www.cde.ca.gov/sp/se/sppapr-overviewfeedback.asp)). The universal financial-aid mandate requires every senior to file a FAFSA or CADAA, or sign an opt-out ([PPIC](https://www.ppic.org/publication/implementing-californias-universal-financial-aid-application-policy/)). A conditionally admitted kindergartner who misses an immunization deadline is excluded from school after a 10-school-day notice ([Murrieta Valley USD AR 5141.31](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1747664160/murrietak12caus/nbtyxoeotvglsf5jzcb4/514131ar.pdf)). In nearly every case the district's legal duty is to *offer* and to *document its attempts*. A family that does not understand, does not receive the notice, or is afraid can stall the process while the district stays technically compliant. That is why the losses are so poorly measured: the systems record what the district did, not what the family failed to finish.

Two findings from the table carry most of the argument. First, the processes with the most money (attendance, the count) are also the ones a district can verify from its own certified state filings. That makes outcome-based pricing possible. Second, the process with the strongest evidence that help works (FAFSA) pays families, not districts. The pitch has to join the two: the evidence comes from aid and benefits, and the payment comes from attendance and the count.

## Attendance and the count carry the money; the rest carry the law and the family

### Attendance: 1.14 million chronically absent, paid by the day

Chronic absence in California peaked at **30.0% in 2021-22** and has fallen every year since, to 24.9%, then 20.4%, then **19.4% in 2024-25**. The decline is slowing: down 5.1 points, then 4.5, then 1.0 ([PACE](https://edpolicyinca.org/publications/unpacking-californias-chronic-absence-crisis-through-2024-25)). The rest of the gap sits in high-poverty schools. In 2024-25, **67% of schools with at least 75% socioeconomically disadvantaged students had high or extreme chronic absence, against 2% of the most affluent schools**. Kindergarten (23%) and high school (24%) are the worst grades. The statewide attendance rate was 93.4% in 2024-25 and 94.5% in 2025-26, against a 95.7% pre-pandemic average ([LAO, May 2026](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/5178)). By my estimate, the 1.2-point gap that remains in 2025-26 is about 70,000 ADA, or roughly **$0.9–1.0B a year** at $13,705 per ADA. That is an estimate, not a published figure.

Families can fix much of this, but not all of it. In a national survey of 5,003 parents, the most common recent non-illness reason for an absence was a medical appointment (21%). Parents of chronically absent children were far more likely to accept tiredness (39% vs 22%) or caregiving (39% vs 20%) as reasons ([Ad Council](https://transitionca.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/acri-studentabsenteeism-report-final.pdf)). Parents also do not know the count: in a California K-5 trial, parents underestimated their child's absences by **5.1 days** ([Robinson et al. 2018](https://toddrogers.scholars.harvard.edu/resource/robinsonleedearingrogers2018reducingabsenteeismintheearlygradespdf)). About half of all absences in California are excused (51.8% in 2024-25), and the statutory truancy-letter sequence counts only unexcused ones, so it never sees half the problem ([CDE](https://www.cde.ca.gov/ds/cm/dqeoy25.asp)). No survey splits absent days into fixable barriers versus disengagement. Any figure for the "addressable share" is a guess.

The evidence is solid for one-way information and absent for two-way help. Personalized mail cut absences by 0.53 days (7.7%) in 10 California districts, at about $10.69 per day recovered. The effect was larger for English learners (−0.83 vs −0.39 days, p=.067) and low-income students (−1.02 vs −0.42, p=.041) ([Robinson et al.](https://toddrogers.scholars.harvard.edu/resource/robinsonleedearingrogers2018reducingabsenteeismintheearlygradespdf)). In Philadelphia, letters showing a child's total absences recovered 1.1 days and cut chronic absence by 10% or more. The authors founded the company that sells this intervention ([Nature Human Behaviour](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0328-1)). At scale the effect almost disappeared: across 131,312 students the effect was **0.07 days (d=0.02)** ([Blueprints](https://blueprintsprograms.org/programs/1544999999/everyday-intervention)). Mentoring recovers more, 4.2 days in grades 5–7 under Check & Connect, but costs about $400 per day and had no effect in grades 1–4 ([IPR WP-20-40](https://www.ipr.northwestern.edu/documents/working-papers/2020/wp-20-40.pdf)). Vendor attendance claims (AllHere's "17%", EveryDay Labs DC's "6.3%", DeKalb's "55%") have no independent evaluation ([Attendance Works](https://www.attendanceworks.org/using-technologies-apps-to-promote-better-attendance/); [EveryDay Labs](https://www.everydaylabs.com/post/research-zone-big-attendance-wins-for-dc-public-schools-dc-public-charter-schools); [AJC](https://www.ajc.com/education/can-automated-messages-help-boost-low-attendance-in-metro-Atlanta-schools/N7CCBSUNUVH5RG7QD66AZRXMDE/)). The closest test of a family-side helper is Rhode Island's Edia trial, launched August 2026. It texts caregivers in their language, asks what is in the way and routes cases to staff, across 67 randomized schools with a RAND evaluation. It has no results yet ([RIDE](https://ride.ri.gov/press-releases/ride-and-edia-launch-first-its-kind-statewide-attendance-partnership-better-engage-students-and-families)). That trial is both a competitor and a likely source of the first independent effect size.

### The unduplicated count: high value per student, no published gap

At 2024-25 rates, one more counted student is worth **$2,035–$2,492 a year** in supplemental grant. In a district above the 55% threshold, the concentration grant adds **$6,615–$8,099**, for a marginal value of about **$9,400–$10,600** (my calculation from [CDE rates](https://www.cde.ca.gov/FG/aa/pa/pa2425rates.asp)). The count is pooled over three years: the current and two prior years' unduplicated counts are divided by those years' total enrollment ([CDE P-1 notes](https://www.cde.ca.gov/FG/AA/PA/calc19p1.asp)). A student counted this October therefore raises funding in this year and the next two, about a third each year. The first cash arrives with the February P-1 payment.

Several tempting claims here are not supported. **There is no published statewide estimate of eligible-but-uncounted students.** At the aggregate level California counts *more* students as low-income than Census poverty data would predict: 60% FRPM in 2019 against an estimated 34% of students under 185% of the poverty line. The universal-meals dip in counted students peaked at about 3% below 2019 in 2021 and **had fully closed by 2023-24** ([PPIC](https://www.ppic.org/?p=82904)). The defensible version is narrower. PACE notes that the SNAP-based measure "still depends on participation in means-tested programs, which have lower participation rates among immigrant families" ([PACE](https://edpolicyinca.org/newsroom/why-california-should-retire-free-or-reduced-price-meal-measure-and-what-state-should-do)). PPIC found that districts with fewer directly certified students had the larger dips. Matching fails on hyphenated names, mismatched birthdates and incomplete addresses: in 2015, San Diego Unified's local matching added 17,924 students, or 40% of all its direct certifications, beyond the state match ([San Diego County HHSA](https://www.sandiegocounty.gov/content/dam/sdc/hhsa/programs/ssp/social_services_advisory_board/documents/12-8-15%20Final%20Direct%20Certification%20Powerpoint.pdf)). The uncounted are a district-level, bottom-up quantity, concentrated in immigrant and mixed-status families and in failed matches. Nobody has measured it. The homeless count is the exception: the State Auditor found schools identified 270,000 homeless students in 2017-18 against at least 370,000 estimated, and most districts identified under 5% of low-income students as homeless where about 10% was expected ([EdSource](https://edsource.org/2019/schools-fail-to-identify-thousands-of-homeless-children-state-audit-finds/620655)). There has been no audit since to say whether that gap persists.

The upside is real when districts help families directly. East Side Union High School District raised eligibility from 42% to 51% of 22,700 students in one campaign. Staff helped parents fill out forms in three languages, reassured undocumented parents and built sign-up into ninth-grade enrollment. The campaign was worth about $800,000 the next year ([EdSource 2014](https://edsource.org/2014/recruiting-students-for-meals-program-pays-off-for-east-side-union/63558)). That campaign predates Medi-Cal direct certification (2017), so there is likely less slack today. Two further facts shape the market. CDE bars cafeteria funds from paying for alternative-income-form work, so any campaign comes out of the general fund. No paid vendor running income-form campaigns was found ([CDE](https://www.cde.ca.gov/FG/AA/PA/altincomeforms.asp)).

### FAFSA and CADAA: the best-evidenced gap, paid to families

California's universal aid-application policy raised completion from 45% (2021-22) to 69% combined FAFSA/CADAA in its first year. In that year **just over 25% of seniors neither applied nor opted out**, with counselors carrying 400–500 students each ([PPIC](https://www.ppic.org/publication/implementing-californias-universal-financial-aid-application-policy/)). The class of 2026 set a record above 70% and 325,000 completers, against a state target of 80% by 2030 ([EdSource](https://edsource.org/updates/californias-class-of-2026-sets-new-record-for-completed-fafsa-applications)). CADAA filings fell for a third straight year in 2025 ([EdSource](https://edsource.org/2025/fafsa-application-rise-2025/741082)). At Hoover High in Fresno, CADAA submissions rose 89% between March and June, after the Cal Grant priority deadline. The author attributes the gap to counselor capacity and fear of data misuse, "not due to lack of interest or eligibility" ([Pasillas 2025](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2t6989kr/qt2t6989kr.pdf)). California students left **nearly $550M in Pell unclaimed** in the 2024 cycle, and a 10-point rise in completion would recover about $145M ([ACE](https://www.higheredtoday.org/2025/05/05/pell-grant-dollars-are-left-unclaimed/)). The money goes to families, not to the district's budget. What the district gets is a compliance mandate, a completion record it can verify in WebGrants, and PPIC's recommendation that completion be added to the California School Dashboard.

### Special education: clocks that start only when a parent signs

Because the 60-day clock starts at consent, the federal timeliness measure (Indicator 11) **leaves out, by design, the time between a referral and a signed plan**. That interval is the family-side delay, and it is unpublished; it lives in SELPA systems such as SEIS and SIRAS. California remained "Needs assistance (two or more consecutive years)" in OSEP's 2026 determinations ([OSEP](https://sites.ed.gov/idea/files/ideafactsheet-determinations-2026.pdf)). The statewide Indicator 11 percentage sits in a Word file the research could not retrieve; that is the most important missing number in this area. Disputes are frequent and mostly private. In FY2024-25, OAH received **4,894 filings**. Only 75 cases ended in a decision; about 4,200 settled ([OAH Q4 FY24-25](https://www.dgs.ca.gov/-/media/Divisions/OAH/Special-Education/Documents-for-Resource-Tab/Quarterly-Reports/24-25/Quarterly-Data-Report-Q4-FY2425Acc.pdf)). A single litigated preschool speech case against LAUSD drew a fee request of about $875,000 ([CSBA](https://publications.csba.org/reports/ela/2025-annual-report/special-education-standard-for-fape/)). The communication failures are documented case by case. Parents waited 6–12 months for translated IEPs. An interpreter error led a team to drop a child's occupational therapy. An Inland Empire mother "unknowingly consented to ending speech services for her non-verbal child." The 30-day translation deadline in SB 445 was dropped after the Assembly estimated it would cost districts $6–16M a year ([EdSource 2023](https://edsource.org/2023/immigrant-parents-report-faulty-slow-translation-of-special-education-documents/700531); [EdSource 2024](https://edsource.org/2024/new-law-moves-toward-better-translation-of-special-ed-documents-but-families-want-more/721022)). No source measures what share of disputes begin with a communication failure. The only family-side intervention evidence is a 19-parent navigator pilot that measured parents' knowledge, not services received ([Vanderbilt](https://discoveries.vanderbilthealth.com/2024/02/parents-helping-parents-navigate-autism/)). The fair claim is that families attend but cannot act, not that a helper reduces litigation.

### Medicaid consent and CYBHI: small dollars, a pure family gate

California's school Medi-Cal billing program (LEA BOP) paid **$98.4M in FY2020-21**, down from $149.5M at its 2014-15 peak ([DHCS](https://www.dhcs.ca.gov/providers-partners/lea-bop-paid-claims-data-reports/)). Nationally, about a third of districts report that 26–50% of consent forms are incomplete, and Chicago loses an estimated $1M a year, or 3–5% of its claims, to missing consent ([K-12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/news/Parental-consent-for-school-Medicaid-reimbursements/639774/)). Applying Chicago's rate to California gives roughly **$3–5M a year statewide**, an estimate. CYBHI is the bigger structural opening but has paid out very little. By October 2025, more than 500 LEAs had enrolled, 55 had submitted claims, and **$1.8M had been reimbursed**, against about $400M in one-time readiness grants ([DHCS](https://www.dhcs.ca.gov/newsroom-office-of-communications/november-10-2025/)). Plans must pay without prior authorization or network contracts ([DHCS CYBHI requirements](https://www.dhcs.ca.gov/hm/CYBHI/Documents/CYBHI-Fee-Schedule-Program-Requirements-August-2025.pdf)), so the barriers that remain are operational, including the per-student insurance file that only a family can supply. This line is clean to verify, because claims either pay or they don't, but on its own it is small.

### Benefits and health: the family's money, the school's address book

The largest dollar gaps at the edge go to families, not districts. **More than 330,000 California children lost Medi-Cal through March 2024**, most of them still eligible. Nearly half of procedural losses involved families who never received the renewal packet ([Georgetown CCF](https://ccf.georgetown.edu/?p=48576)), and about a third of people cut off read Spanish ([NHeLP](https://healthlaw.org/california-revisited-where-the-golden-state-stands-after-the-unwinding/?state=All)). CalFresh misses more than one in three eligible Californians, 2.7 million people and about $3.5B a year in federal benefits ([Nourish California](https://nourishca.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/2024-Lost-Dollars-Empty-Plates.pdf)). The average household benefit is $332 a month ([LAO](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/4933/3)). In 2025, **nearly a fifth of SUN Bucks cards were never activated and $118M went unspent** ([Times of San Diego](https://timesofsandiego.com/health/2026/10/07/california-families-are-running-out-of-time-to-use-120-per-kid-benefit-on-groceries/)). In community-eligibility schools a child needs individual eligibility for SUN Bucks, so the only route for an unmatched family is a form returned to the school ([CDE Flash 269](https://www.cde.ca.gov/DS/sp/cl/calpadsupdflash269.asp)). These gaps link back to district money. A Medi-Cal match at or below 185% of the poverty line counts every school-age child in the household without any form ([CDE SNP-02-2025](https://www.cde.ca.gov/LS/nu/sn/mbsnp022025.asp)), so a renewal finished for a family can also be a student counted for the district. Pressure is about to rise: federal changes could remove an estimated 737,000 CalFresh recipients from June 2026 ([Gov. Newsom](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2026/05/21/california-launches-sun-bucks-for-summer-2026-to-help-fight-childhood-food-insecurity/)), and H.R. 1's more frequent Medicaid renewals begin January 1, 2027 ([Georgetown CCF](https://ccf.georgetown.edu/2026/02/26/new-state-by-state-medicaid-and-chip-tracker-shows-declining-enrollment-as-h-r-1-cuts-loom/)).

Health follow-up fails at the same point: a form that must travel home and come back. In the Florida vision trial, sending a note home asking families to see a doctor had **no effect**. Screening plus free glasses delivered at school raised reading pass rates 2.5 points and math 3.4 points, and **25% of diagnosed children still got no glasses because the permission form was not returned, the child was absent on clinic day, or both** ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/providing-vision-screening-and-free-eyeglasses-elementary-schools-united-states)). California's kindergarten dental assessment has no public compliance rate; an Assembly audit request says "a majority of parents are not complying" in many districts ([JLAC letter](https://legaudit.legislature.ca.gov/media/510)). In ten Milwaukee schools, physician orders for asthma medication were on file for only 11–22% of children with asthma ([The Hospitalist](https://community.the-hospitalist.org/index.php/content/paperwork-snarls-stand-between-kids-and-school-asthma-medications)).

### Enrollment, immunization and transitions: the operational tail

No California district publishes the share of families who finish re-registration before day one. National examples range from 30% a few days before the deadline (Beaumont, 2020) to 56% a week out (Shelby County) to 75% in the first year of online forms (DeSoto) ([Chalkbeat](https://www.chalkbeat.org/tennessee/2015/8/3/21095424/just-over-half-of-shelby-county-students-are-registered-a-week-before-classes-start/); [12NewsNow](https://12newsnow.com/article/news/local/only-30-percent-of-beaumont-isd-students-registered-for-upcoming-2020-2021-school-year/502-382bcf91-472f-44f2-b827-e6173d3efe63); [PowerSchool](https://www1.powerschool.com/case-studies/how-desoto-county-schools-saves-over-260000-with-online-enrollment/)). Tustin Unified celebrated four schools reaching 100% around September ([Tustin USD](https://www.tustin.k12.ca.us/about-us/news/posts/~board/tustin-unified-school-district-news/post/heideman-a-team-first-to-100-data-confirmation)). In 2024-25, 11,517 kindergartners (1.9%) entered school conditionally on immunizations and 1.2% were overdue and subject to exclusion. CDPH does not report how many were actually excluded ([CDPH](https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CID/DCDC/CDPH%20Document%20Library/Immunization/2024-25KindergartenSummaryReport.pdf)). Records follow movers poorly: only 65.8% of foster youth and 79.5% of homeless students complete a full year at one school ([ACSA EdCal](https://edcal.acsa.org/state-releases-first-ever-stability-rate-report)), and an AP/Stanford analysis found more than 150,000 California students missing from every system after 2020 ([Fortune/AP](https://fortune.com/2023/02/09/how-many-high-school-students-missing-after-pandemic-ap-stanford-missing-240000/)). In transitions, TK enrollment more than doubled to 177,570, but the share of eligible children enrolling fell, and LPI lists "paperwork challenges" among causes that are "not currently understood" ([LPI](https://learningpolicyinstitute.org/product/ca-universal-prek-expansion-enroll-brief)). Latino students are 49% of dual enrollees against 57% of high schoolers ([PPIC](https://www.ppic.org/publication/fact-sheet-dual-enrollment-in-california/)). ELO-P spends $4.6B a year with no state participation data at all ([LAO](https://lao.ca.gov/Education/EdBudget/Details/1141)). These are real failures, but they are thinly measured and mostly not verifiable. They belong in the story, not in the pricing.

## Help beats information wherever the two were tested head to head

The pattern across programs is consistent. In Pennsylvania, nine-month SNAP enrollment among Medicaid-enrolled seniors was **5.8% with no contact, 10.5% with letters, and 17.6% with letters plus phone help applying**. Each added enrollee cost about $20 with letters alone and about $60 with assistance ([MIT Economics](https://economics.mit.edu/research/publications/take-and-targeting-experimental-evidence-snap)). In the H&R Block FAFSA trial, tax preparers pre-filled the form and walked families through it in about eight minutes. FAFSA filing rose **15.7 points for dependents and 26.7 points for independents without college**, and college enrollment rose 8.1 points. The **information-only arm, a personalized aid estimate with no help filing, had no measurable effect**. Assistance cost about $88 per participant ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/role-application-assistance-and-information-college-decisions-results-hr-block-fafsa)). Florida's vision trial shows the same result for a school-health form: a note home did nothing, and delivering the service at school worked. At scale, one-way information disappears. Bird et al. sent FAFSA nudges to more than 800,000 students through the Common App and a large state and found **no effect on aid or enrollment overall or in any subgroup**, even though more than 40% of students in the state arm replied. The authors point to the distant sender and generic one-way messages ([NBER w26158](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26158.pdf)). More broadly, nudge units' real trials average 1.4 points, against 8.7 points in published papers ([Conversable Economist on DellaVigna & Linos](https://conversableeconomist.com/2023/07/21/second-thoughts-about-nudge-based-behavioral-policies/)).

Two results sharpen what "assistance" has to mean, and both bear directly on product design. First, **conversation is not the same as completion**. In a New Orleans early-childhood verification trial, personalized two-way texts from a named staff member raised verification about 7 points, the same as formal one-way reminders, even though 89% of parents replied ([ERA-New Orleans](https://educationresearchalliancenola.org/files/publications/20200623-Technical-Report-Gerry-et-al-Increasing-Access-to-Head-Start-Through-Text-Messages.pdf)). Wisconsin's Medicaid renewal trial found that changing the written outreach (postcard vs text, one text vs two) did nothing. A cheap recorded call added 1.0 point overall and **2.1 points for children**, and procedural loss fell 21 points among people who reached a navigator, a group that chose to call ([J-PAL Wisconsin](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/node/8122780)). Adding texts on top of three existing reminders in Virginia did nothing ([OES](https://oes.gsa.gov/projects/2406-increasing-medicaid-renewals-with-texts/)). Reminders saturate quickly; doing the work is where the effect lies. Second, **help works best for people who have already decided**. Georgia State's Pounce chatbot raised on-time enrollment 3.3 points among students who had already committed, with fewer than 1% of messages needing staff and a cost of $7–15 per student, but it had no significant effect across the full sample ([AERA Open](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2332858417749220)). Most school-edge tasks fit this case: the parent already wants the child in school, already wants the glasses, already wants the aid. That also answers the paternalism objection, because a helper that carries out a choice the parent has already made is not steering anyone.

**The messenger and the language decide who is reached.** The SNAP trial found that outreach and assistance mostly drew in less-needy, white, English-speaking enrollees ([NBER w24652](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24652/w24652.pdf)). Help that is not designed for language and trust ends up helping the easiest families first. In California this is the central design problem, not an edge case. **44% of California children (3.7M) have an immigrant parent, and more than 1M have an undocumented parent** ([The Children's Partnership](https://childrenspartnership.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/imm_fam_snapshot_aciac_2026_final_r5b_2026_08_14.pdf)). 39.5% of public-school students speak a language other than English at home ([CDE](https://www.cde.ca.gov/ds/ad/cefelfacts.asp)). About one in four adults in mixed-status families avoided safety-net programs in 2023 for immigration reasons ([Urban Institute](https://digitalgovernmenthub.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Mixed-Status-Families-and-Immigrant-Families-with-Children-Continued-Avoiding-Safety-Net-Programs-in-2023.pdf)). In 2019 only 18% of California immigrant-family adults knew that a child's Medi-Cal enrollment does not count against the parents ([CHCF](https://www.chcf.org/blog/2020/05/18/one-six-california-immigrants-avoid-public-benefits-study-finds/)). Language access, the human layer that is supposed to bridge this, is built around Spanish. The last statewide audit found 91% of required notices translated into Spanish, 54% into Mandarin and 48% into Hmong ([State Auditor](https://information.auditor.ca.gov/pdfs/factsheets/2005-137.pdf)). That audit is two decades old; no newer one exists.

**The school is the trusted door, with one caveat.** Parents trust teachers most for attendance messages (61%), and scolding messages "did not work" ([Ad Council](https://transitionca.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/acri-studentabsenteeism-report-final.pdf)). Bird et al.'s null result is largely a result about a distant sender. Schools also hold what renewal systems lack: a current address and home language, refreshed every year at re-registration. That matters, because in Wisconsin at most 61–78% of calls, texts and postcards reached the right person due to stale contact data, and about half of California's procedural Medi-Cal losses involved a packet that never arrived. The caveat: **15% of California foreign-born parents were asked for a Social Security number or proof of citizenship when enrolling a child in school**, which is illegal in California ([The Children's Partnership](https://childrenspartnership.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/imm_fam_snapshot_aciac_2026_final_r5b_2026_08_14.pdf)). The school door can create fear as well as trust. The design that fits the evidence is the one Mycelium already describes: a helper that belongs to the family and is sponsored by the school, so it borrows the school's trust and contact data without becoming the institution.

**The missing trial is the opportunity.** The rigorous K-12 parent research is almost all *information*: missed-assignment alerts (Bergman: +0.19 SD GPA in an LAUSD school), absence letters, teacher texts that cut summer course failure from 15.8% to 9.3% ([Bergman](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f68410/f68410.pdf); [Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/summer-school-coming-get-parents-involved/)). **No published randomized trial tests assisted completion of a K-12 form by families**, and no study measures an AI agent submitting a form for anyone ([NBER w24652](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24652/w24652.pdf); [Propel](https://www.propel.app/insights/using-ai-to-help-snap-recipients-diagnose-and-restore-lost-benefits/)). The thesis is an extrapolation from SNAP, FAFSA and Medicaid to schools. It is a well-founded extrapolation, and the right move is to say so and offer to run the trial.

## The edge is the business; any single form is a rounding error

The critique deserves a straight answer. Taken one process at a time, the contingency fees are small. California's Medicaid consent loss is about $3–5M a year statewide, so a 20% share is under $1M. In a 10,000-student district, attendance recovery at the effect sizes the trials support is a five-figure fee. Many districts fund on prior-year or three-year-average ADA, so even that fee arrives a year late. Anyone who prices Mycelium as an attendance-nudge vendor will conclude it is a small business, and EveryDay Labs' published pricing ($1–3 per student plus a $15–35K setup fee; [Blueprints](https://blueprintsprograms.org/programs/1544999999/everyday-intervention)) shows where that market sits.

The answer is that the family, not the form, is the unit of work. One family has one set of barriers and one channel, and a single yes can cover several tasks. Some of these links are already in the rules: one alternative income form counts a student, qualifies a community-eligibility child for $120 of SUN Bucks, and unlocks AP and college-application fee waivers. CDE itself tells districts to pitch those bundled benefits ([CDE Flash #254](https://www.cde.ca.gov/ds/sp/cl/calpadsupdflash254.asp)). One Medi-Cal renewal at or below 185% of the poverty line counts every school-age sibling. One conversation about a missed bus is also a chance to catch a lapsed renewal or an unsigned consent. At a 20–25% share, that district yields roughly **$40,000–$300,000 a year**. That is more than districts pay for messaging platforms (ParentSquare lists $6 per student; one Utah district pays about $137,600 a year: [ParentSquare](https://www.parentsquare.com/?p=12041); [Jordan SD](https://purchasing.jordandistrict.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/Summary-of-Award-24TH17P5-Parent-Guardian-Communication-Info-Systems.pdf)). It is in the range of one or two liaison salaries ($25–49 an hour posted in California: [EdJoin LAUSD](https://www.edjoin.org/Home/JobPosting/1965675); [EdJoin Solana Beach](https://www.edjoin.org/Home/JobPosting/2254770)). It is far below mentoring at about $1,700 per student. The statewide figures give the outer bounds, all estimates. Every point of statewide attendance is worth roughly $0.8B. The gap to pre-pandemic attendance is worth about $0.9–1.0B a year. Each 0.1% of enrollment newly counted is about 5,850 students, or $12–60M spread over three years. California already spends an estimated $0.6–0.7B a year on the explicit school–family edge before counting any liaison or clerk salaries, mainly the $4.1B Community Schools program, which averages roughly $550–600M a year across its grant window ([CDE CCSPP](https://www.cde.ca.gov/Ci/gs/hs/ccspp24execsummary.asp)). Beyond California, the same structure scales: Title I is $18.2B nationally ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R48893.html)), school Medicaid is $3–4B a year ([K-12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/news/Parental-consent-for-school-Medicaid-reimbursements/639774/)), and unclaimed Pell was $4.4B for the class of 2024 ([NCAN](https://www.ncan.org/Web/News/698621.aspx)). The union-fund Part B line shows that the same mechanism, help plus a yes plus payment on verified outcomes, works outside schools. A reader should take three things from these numbers. The count is the line with the most leverage and the least evidence. Attendance is the line with the cleanest verification. The family's own benefits are worth more than the district's revenue, which is why the helper can credibly say it works for the family.

## Five constraints decide when, and whether, the money arrives

**FERPA posture has to be one thing, stated clearly.** A district-contracted tool is a FERPA "school official" only if it performs a function the district would otherwise staff, stays "under the direct control of the school," and does not redisclose personal information ([FPF](https://fpf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Ed_AI-legal-compliance.pdf)). California's AB 1584 requires every such contract to say that pupil records remain the LEA's property and under its control, to bar any use beyond the contract, and to require deletion at the end ([AB 1584](https://www.leginfo.ca.gov/pub/13-14/bill/asm/ab_1551-1600/ab_1584_bill_20140829_enrolled.html)). An agent authorized by the parent, using the parent's own FERPA right to consent to disclosure, sits mostly outside the school-official exception. No regulator or court has addressed a parent-side AI agent. The current /schools copy combines the two in one sentence: "The thread stays with the family, under FERPA's school-official rules." By my reading of AB 1584, that is a tension: records a school official keeps for the district belong to the district. The workable structure is a two-role design: the school-official role for the roster and the offer, then parent consent for each action, which is what the per-action YES already provides, for anything that leaves the school or concerns another agency. Counsel should confirm it before it is put in writing.

**LAUSD and AllHere set the failure pattern districts will check for.** LAUSD signed a $6M contract, paid about $3M, and took "Ed" offline about three months after launch when AllHere collapsed. A whistleblower alleged student data was included in every prompt and routed to servers in seven countries, and the inspector general opened an investigation ([The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/whistleblower-l-a-schools-chatbot-misused-student-data-as-tech-co-crumbled/)). A teacher said Ed mostly surfaced information already on the district website ([The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/la-unified-faces-criticism-after-collapse-of-splashy-ai-tool-ed/)). The lessons line up with the evidence above. Do tasks rather than answer questions. Send only the minimum data in any prompt. Keep data in the US. Have no platform fee that leaves a district out of pocket if the vendor fails. Meet the government-assistant accuracy standard: GOV.UK Chat reached 90% accuracy and still ships source checks with human escalation ([UKAuthority](https://www.ukauthority.com/articles/gov-uk-ai-chat-completes-public-pilot)).

**Enrolling a student is not the same as serving one.** LCFF regulations (5 CCR §15496) require supplemental and concentration dollars to "increase or improve services" for the students who generate them, and LCAP actions funded that way must be "principally directed" toward those students. This rule is regulatory context not documented in the research notes, so confirm the wording before using it. Districts already present counted dollars as funding services: Oxnard Union tells families each unsubmitted form means lost Chromebooks, tutoring and counseling ([Oxnard Union](https://www.oxnardunion.org/departments/business-services/nutrition-services/lcff)). The rule cuts both ways. A pitch of "we get you more money" invites the question "for what?" The helper itself, a translated, task-completing service aimed at low-income, English-learner and foster families, is plausibly an action principally directed at unduplicated pupils. That would give it a natural budget line, though no retrieved source names the line districts actually use for such vendors.

**Hold-harmless timing decides when attendance money shows up.** Districts, but not charters, are funded on the greatest of current-year, prior-year or three-year-average ADA. In 2025-26, **43% of districts were funded on current-year ADA, 19% on prior-year and 38% on the three-year average**. The hold harmless adds about 74,000 ADA (1.5%) statewide ([LAO](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/5178)). For 57% of districts, a gain this year raises next year's funding floor and rolling average rather than this year's payment. FCMAT warns that three-year averaging "offers minimal mitigation going forward" ([FCMAT](https://www.fcmat.org/publicationsreports/basc-conf-presentation-fall-2024.pdf)). Pricing on funded ADA, as the /schools page does, is the right answer. The cushion also shrinks as the pandemic years leave the average, which is a reason to start now.

**The count has a calendar.** The count is a Census Day snapshot with forms due October 31, certified in December, pooled over three years and first paid in February ([CDE](https://www.cde.ca.gov/FG/AA/PA/altincomeforms.asp)). A pilot that starts in the spring cannot move the count until the following October, and even then the gain arrives in thirds. Community-eligibility schools re-establish their base year at least every four years and collect forms only from new students in between, which limits how much annual form work exists. AB 176 lets districts count currently enrolled students found eligible after the base year. In a district below 55% unduplicated, a counted student is worth about $2,000, not $9,750.

### What the first district pilot should measure

Each unsupported claim above corresponds to a number a pilot can produce. The pilot should be staggered school by school so that schools not yet offered serve as a comparison group, and each student should be measured against their own prior year.

| Gap in the evidence | What to measure | System of record |
|---|---|---|
| No K-12 trial of assisted completion | Completion rates in offered vs not-yet-offered schools, for each form type | SIS completion flags, CALPADS, WebGrants |
| No published eligible-but-uncounted level | Families with no FRPM record and no direct-certification match who qualify when helped; the share found through new Medi-Cal/CalFresh enrollment vs a form | CALPADS Fall 1 (181/182), state match file |
| No trial of two-way attendance help | Days recovered per engaged family against their own prior year; ADA at P-2; results by language | SIS daily codes, P-2 certification |
| Referral-to-consent delay is invisible | Days from referral to signed assessment plan; IEP attendance; translated summary delivered before the meeting | SEIS/SIRAS |
| Consent and insurance gaps unmeasured in CA | Medi-Cal consents and CYBHI insurance files completed; claims paid | LEA BOP / Carelon claims |
| Assistance reaches the less needy first | YES rate and completion rate by home language, beyond Spanish, and by unduplicated status | Helper logs joined to the SIS |
| AI accuracy and safety | Escalation rate, error rate on submitted forms, time to completion, cost per completed task | Helper logs, staff audit sample |

## Conclusion

The research supports the thesis and changes where its weight falls. The family-side gaps are real, they cluster in the same households, and the strongest evidence for fixing them comes from outside schools. SNAP, FAFSA, Medicaid renewals and Florida's glasses all point the same way: carrying out a choice the family has already made works, and telling them about it does not. That makes the honest pitch stronger than the inflated one. Mycelium does not need a statewide uncounted-student figure or a vendor's attendance headline. It needs to say that the mechanism is proven in adjacent programs, that the school is the door with current contact data and a parent's trust, and that the district's own certified filings will show whether it worked within a fiscal year.

The new strategic point is that the processes are tied together in the rules. A Medi-Cal renewal can count a sibling, one income form can unlock SUN Bucks and fee waivers, and one conversation about a missed bus can surface the lapsed coverage behind the absences. That linkage is why the edge works as a business when each form on its own is small, and it is also the part no competitor sees, because attendance vendors, messaging platforms and benefits navigators each own one slice. The first pilot that measures completion by language, the uncounted students it finds and days recovered against each student's own prior year, staggered by school, would be the first trial of its kind. That trial is the moat.
